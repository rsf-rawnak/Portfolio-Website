Japan Dream

~3 min read Long-term goal

People usually assume the Japan goal comes from anime or pop culture, and I won't pretend that interest is zero — but it's not why I'm actually building toward it. The real reason is more specific: Japan is one of the few places in the world where industrial manufacturing and applied machine learning aren't separate worlds. Precision machining, robotics, quality control systems — the exact domain my thesis lives in — are treated as a serious, ongoing engineering frontier there, not a niche subfield.

That's a rare fit. A lot of ML career paths point toward consumer software, ads, recommendation systems — important work, but not what pulls me. Industrial AI, the kind that watches a machine's vibration signature and tells you it's about to fail before it does, is the work I actually want to be doing for a career, not just for a thesis. Japan's manufacturing sector is where that work is happening at scale.

So the plan isn't "move abroad" in the abstract. It's a specific, staged pathway, and I'd rather lay it out plainly than leave it as a vague aspiration:

The actual plan

1. A government-funded research scholarship pathway. Rather than self-funding a master's or going through a private language school first, I'm building toward a research scholarship route — the kind that places you as a research student under a professor before formal graduate enrollment, fully funded, no tuition or living-cost burden. That route depends on having a real research profile going in, which is exactly why the thesis and the portfolio projects aren't separate from this goal — they're the application.

2. Language proficiency, not just conversational Japanese. I'm currently at an elementary level and actively working up through the JLPT tiers — the target is reaching a working-proficiency level before applying, since a research placement means functioning in a Japanese academic and professional environment, not just getting around as a tourist.

3. A research plan that connects directly to my thesis work. Scholarship applications like this aren't judged on a personal statement — they're judged on a concrete research proposal and a professor willing to supervise it. That means identifying labs and researchers in Japan working on industrial anomaly detection or manufacturing AI, and building a proposal that extends what I've already done in my thesis rather than starting from zero.

4. The residency arc after that. Scholarship → research student status → formal graduate enrollment if the route requires it → work visa through an industrial AI role → permanent residency → and eventually citizenship. Each stage has its own requirements and timelines in Japan, and I'm treating this as a multi-year plan, not a single application I either win or lose.

None of this is separate from the engineering work. The thesis builds the research profile. The portfolio projects prove I can execute independently. The language study is the part that turns "qualified on paper" into "actually employable in Japan." It's all one plan, worked from multiple angles at once.

I don't have every stage locked down yet, and I'm not going to pretend the timeline is guaranteed — scholarship cycles, visa policy, and admissions committees are outside my control. But having a specific, staged plan instead of a vague "I'd like to work in Japan someday" is what makes the unglamorous parts of right now — another semester of coursework, another round of grammar drills, debugging a broken feature pipeline at 2am — feel like they're building toward something concrete instead of just passing time.
